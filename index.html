About the ofrenda

Ofrendas are altars created during Día de los Muertos to honor and remember loved ones who have died. Traditions vary by family, community and region.

Marigolds are often used for their bright color and fragrance. Candles can represent light and guidance. Water may be offered to refresh the spirit. Salt is associated with purification. Pan de muerto is a traditional seasonal bread. Papel picado adds festive color and movement. Photos, favorite foods and personal belongings help celebrate the individual being remembered.

Items such as cigarettes, alcohol, fast food, music and pet memorials are included here as personal remembrance options, not as required or universal elements of an ofrenda.

Your ofrenda can be personal. There is no requirement to add a photo or dedicate it to a specific person.

What do the items mean?

Learn about some of the traditional elements commonly included on an ofrenda.

Cempasúchils / Marigolds

Cempasúchils, or marigolds, are the flowers most closely associated with Día de Muertos. They were also used in Indigenous Aztec rituals connected with honoring the dead, including traditions associated with Mictecacihuatl, known as the Lady of the Dead.

The vibrant flowers are used to attract the spirits, while their strong scent is traditionally believed to help guide the dead to the altar.

Water

Indigenous traditions were greatly influenced by the earth and its elements during Día de Muertos observances. A cup or bowl of water may be left on an ofrenda to represent purification and to help cleanse the souls traveling back to the land of the living.

Water is also offered to quench the thirst of the dead after their journey.

Candles

Candles represent the element of fire. They are placed on an ofrenda to light a path for the souls to follow. Traditionally, the light helps guide souls from the darkness of the underworld to the world of the living.

Candles may also be placed in a row leading toward the ofrenda.

Food

Plates of food are placed on ofrendas as offerings for the souls who come to visit. In traditional beliefs, souls make a long journey to the living world and may become hungry along the way. Families often prepare a loved one's favorite dishes and place them on the ofrenda.

Corn may also be included as a tribute and an expression of gratitude to ancestors connected with the harvest.

Papel Picado

Colorful strings of papel picado are hung on ofrendas to represent wind and air. The cut openings allow air to flow through the paper, symbolizing a soul's journey to the world of the living. Papel picado also brings color and movement to the ofrenda.

Incense

Incense, often copal, may be burned on an ofrenda. Its scent and smoke are traditionally used to help draw the souls toward the altar. Incense is also associated with purification and cleansing the space.

Pan de Muerto

Pan de muerto is one of the foods most commonly associated with Día de Muertos and serves as an offering of food for the dead. Its decorative forms are often interpreted as symbols associated with death, bones, or the cycle of life and death.

Its traditions developed through the blending of Indigenous practices and Spanish colonial influences.

Sugar Skulls

Calaveras, especially sugar skulls, are commonly placed on ofrendas as colorful representations of death and remembrance. Skull imagery has deep roots in Indigenous Mexican cultures and has become one of the most recognizable symbols associated with Día de Muertos.

Picture Frames

Photos provide a visual representation of the loved ones being honored on the ofrenda. They identify who the altar is dedicated to and symbolically welcome those loved ones back for the celebration.

Personal Items

Other items may be added because they were meaningful to the person or pet being remembered. These might include a favorite food or drink, music, a book, a special object, or something else they loved during their life.
